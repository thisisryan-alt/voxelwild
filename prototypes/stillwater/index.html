        Each part of Stillwater is built from a finding in stress, attention or perception research. These are findings about the ingredients, not a clinical trial of this game.

        • 6 breaths / min · cyclic sigh · box · 4-7-8

          The island breathes slowly

          The tide, the foam line, the lantern, the cat and the surf follow the breathing pattern you choose. Breathing near six times a minute raises heart-rate variability and is linked to lower anxiety, and a long exhale favours the calming side of the nervous system. In a month-long trial, five minutes a day of cyclic sighing lifted mood more than mindfulness meditation. Box and 4-7-8 breathing are popular but less studied.

          Lehrer & Gevirtz (2014), Frontiers in Psychology · Zaccaro et al. (2018), Frontiers in Human Neuroscience · Balban et al. (2023), Cell Reports Medicine
        • water · sky · green

          Blue space and living plants

          Scenes with water are rated the most restorative of all landscapes, and views of nature speed recovery after stress compared with urban views. The palette stays in low-saturation blues and greens, which people rate as the most pleasant and least arousing colours.

          White et al. (2010), J. Environmental Psychology · Ulrich et al. (1991), J. Environmental Psychology · Valdez & Mehrabian (1994), J. Experimental Psychology: General
        • seven islands · seasons · weather

          Soft fascination, and a world to explore

          Attention Restoration Theory holds that gently interesting things hold attention without effort and let tired, effortful attention recover. It also names being away, a sense of a place apart from daily demands, and extent, a coherent world big enough to wander. Seven islands, a slow row between them, seasons, weather and visitors give the world that depth without asking anything of you.

          Kaplan (1995), J. Environmental Psychology · Berman, Jonides & Kaplan (2008), Psychological Science
        • koi · seals · reef fish · whale

          Watching animals

          In an aquarium study, heart rate dropped and mood improved as more fish were added to the tank. The sea here has koi, reef fish, seals, a turtle, leaping fish and the occasional whale.

          Cracknell et al. (2016), Environment and Behavior
        • fractal dimension ≈ 1.3–1.5

          Mid-complexity fractals

          People prefer, and show lower physiological stress when viewing, fractal patterns of moderate complexity, the range found in clouds and tree canopies. Trees grow by repeated branching, and clouds form from layered noise.

          Taylor et al. (2011), Frontiers in Human Neuroscience · Hagerhall et al. (2008), Perception
        • surf · rain · wind · birds · crickets

          Natural sound

          After a stressor, nature sounds bring skin conductance back to baseline faster than traffic noise, and they shift brain activity toward relaxed, outward-directed attention. Each layer has its own slider.

          Alvarsson, Wiens & Nilsson (2010), Int. J. Environmental Research and Public Health · Gould van Praag et al. (2017), Scientific Reports
        • campfire

          Firelight

          Watching a crackling fire lowered blood pressure in a controlled study, and longer viewing helped more. Build a campfire and sit near it at night.

          Lynn (2014), Evolutionary Psychology
        • pentatonic · slow attack · rests

          Slow music with pauses

          Slow-tempo music lowers heart and breathing rate, and pauses between pieces lower them further. The pads swell in over five seconds, stay in a consonant pentatonic key, and leave silences where only the sea plays.

          Bernardi, Porta & Sleight (2006), Heart
        • aurora · whale · shooting stars

          Small moments of awe

          Short weekly “awe walks” increased joy and other positive emotions in a controlled trial. The island keeps a few rare sights for you to come across.

          Sturm et al. (2022), Emotion
        • something good

          Gratitude

          Writing down a few good things each day raised happiness and eased low mood for months in a randomised trial. Each one you write here becomes a glowing flower.

          Seligman et al. (2005), American Psychologist · Emmons & McCullough (2003), J. Personality and Social Psychology
        • let something go

          Setting a thought down

          People who wrote their thoughts on paper and physically threw them away relied on those thoughts less afterward. The lantern is a symbolic version: the words are never saved.

          Briñol, Gascó, Petty & Horcajo (2013), Psychological Science
        • 3 · 5 · 10 · 20 minutes

          Sitting quietly

          Meditation programmes show moderate evidence for easing anxiety and low mood. Sit gives you a short, bounded quiet with a bell at each end.

          Goyal et al. (2014), JAMA Internal Medicine
        • no score · no timer · no failure

          Nothing to lose

          Stress hormones rise most for tasks that feel uncontrollable and judged, so there is no score, clock, failure or audience. Gardening lowered cortisol more than reading in a controlled study, and simple casual games have reduced stress measures in the lab.

          Dickerson & Kemeny (2004), Psychological Bulletin · Van Den Berg & Custers (2011), J. Health Psychology · Russoniello, O’Brien & Parks (2009), J. CyberTherapy & Rehabilitation
