Stillwater

A small island that breathes at six breaths a minute. Plant something, drop a pebble, or just watch the water.

Headphones help. There is nothing to win, no timer, and nothing can go wrong.

Why it calms

Each part of Stillwater is built from a finding in stress, attention or perception research. These are findings about the ingredients, not a clinical trial of this game.

  • 4 s in · 6 s out · 6 breaths / min

    The island breathes slowly

    The tide, the foam line, the lantern and the sound of the waves all follow a 10-second cycle. Breathing near six times a minute raises heart-rate variability and is linked to lower anxiety and arousal, and a longer exhale than inhale favours the calming side of the nervous system. Follow along if you like; it works without you.

    Lehrer & Gevirtz (2014), Frontiers in Psychology · Zaccaro et al. (2018), Frontiers in Human Neuroscience
  • water · sky · green

    Blue space and living plants

    Scenes with water are rated the most restorative of all landscapes, and views of nature speed recovery after stress compared with urban views. The palette stays in low-saturation blues and greens, which people rate as the most pleasant and least arousing colours.

    White et al. (2010), J. Environmental Psychology · Ulrich et al. (1991), J. Environmental Psychology · Valdez & Mehrabian (1994), J. Experimental Psychology: General
  • clouds · koi · fireflies · petals

    Soft fascination

    Attention Restoration Theory holds that gently interesting things, like drifting clouds or fish, hold attention without effort and let the tired, effortful kind of attention recover. Everything here moves slowly and nothing demands a response.

    Kaplan (1995), J. Environmental Psychology · Berman, Jonides & Kaplan (2008), Psychological Science
  • fractal dimension ≈ 1.3–1.5

    Mid-complexity fractals

    People prefer, and show lower physiological stress when viewing, fractal patterns of moderate complexity, the range found in clouds and tree canopies. Trees grow by repeated branching, and clouds form from layered noise.

    Taylor et al. (2011), Frontiers in Human Neuroscience · Hagerhall et al. (2008), Perception
  • waves · wind · birds · crickets

    Natural sound

    After a stressor, nature sounds bring skin conductance back to baseline faster than traffic noise, and they shift brain activity toward relaxed, outward-directed attention. All sound here is synthesised live: surf, wind, birdsong by day and crickets at night.

    Alvarsson, Wiens & Nilsson (2010), Int. J. Environmental Research and Public Health · Gould van Praag et al. (2017), Scientific Reports
  • pentatonic · slow attack · rests

    Slow music with pauses

    Slow-tempo music lowers heart and breathing rate, and pauses between pieces lower them further. The pads swell in over five seconds, sit in a consonant pentatonic key, and leave silences where only the sea plays.

    Bernardi, Porta & Sleight (2006), Heart
  • no score · no timer · no failure

    Nothing to lose

    Stress hormones rise most for tasks that feel uncontrollable and judged. So there is no score, clock, failure or audience. The one task, gardening, lowered cortisol more than reading in a controlled study, and simple casual games have reduced stress measures in lab settings.

    Dickerson & Kemeny (2004), Psychological Bulletin · Van Den Berg & Custers (2011), J. Health Psychology · Russoniello, O’Brien & Parks (2009), J. CyberTherapy & Rehabilitation
